Liquidity constraints in position sizing

The exit floor is not a service promise to clients. It is a constraint we impose on ourselves at the point of sizing, and it costs us yield in precisely the quarters when nobody wants to pay for it.

Type
Essay
Published
Q3 2026

Most portfolios treat liquidity as a property of an instrument: something you look up, note, and move past. We treat it as a property of a position - the pairing of an instrument with a size, held in a particular book, under a particular regime. The same bond is liquid at one size and illiquid at four times that size, and the difference is not discoverable from a fact sheet.

The constraint, stated plainly

Every position must be reducible to cash within five business days under a stressed but orderly market assumption, without the act of reducing it moving the price against the rest of the book. That requirement enters the optimiser as a hard constraint, not a preference or a tie-breaker. A candidate allocation that cannot satisfy it is not down-weighted; it is rejected. The five-day window used throughout this note illustrates the mechanism; each mandate's actual exit floor is set in its investment management agreement, not here.

The stressed assumption matters more than the number of days. A five-day floor computed on calm-market volumes is not a floor at all - it is an average dressed up as a limit. We size against participation rates observed in the worst comparable stretch in the validation window, not the median one.

Why it enters at sizing, not at exit

A liquidity rule applied at the moment of exit is a rule that has already failed. By then the position exists, the size is fixed, and the only remaining choice is how much of the spread to pay. Applying the same rule at sizing converts an unmanageable question - how do we get out of this - into a manageable one: how large is the position allowed to be, given that we have pre-committed to being able to get out of it.

A liquidity rule applied at the moment of exit is a rule that has already failed.

This is also why the constraint is uncomfortable. It binds hardest on the positions that look most attractive, because thin markets are exactly where mispricing survives longest. The floor systematically declines opportunities that a less constrained manager would take, and it does so without knowing whether any individual decline was correct.

How it is enforced

The floor is not a document. It is checked mechanically at two points in the nightly cycle, and a breach stops the cycle rather than raising a flag for someone to read in the morning.

  1. BlendCandidate weights are produced from regime-conditional views blended through Black-Litterman with hierarchical risk parity. Liquidity does not enter yet.
  2. OptimisationThe five-day floor is applied as a hard constraint on every position. Where it binds, the position is capped rather than the constraint relaxed.
  3. RiskThe capped portfolio is re-tested at the book level: can the whole book be reduced by a meaningful fraction within the window, not merely each position in isolation.
  4. ValidationThe resulting rule set is re-run through the rolling walk-forward window before it is allowed into production.

The book-level test in stage three is the one that catches the failure mode people miss. Twenty positions that each individually clear a five-day exit can be, collectively, a single correlated liquidity position that clears nothing - because they would all be exiting into the same buyers on the same day.

What it costs

The honest accounting is that the floor has a running cost and no visible benefit until it is needed. In calm conditions it declines carry, caps positions below the size the model would otherwise take, and excludes instruments entirely. That cost is continuous, measurable, and accepted by design. Whether it is worth paying is not something a calm quarter can answer, and we do not present it as settled.

Scope

The exit floor is an internal sizing constraint. It is not a redemption term, a guarantee of liquidity to the client, or an assurance about any particular exit price. Markets can and do become disorderly beyond the stressed assumption used in sizing. Under those conditions the floor governs how large our positions were before the event, which is the only thing a manager actually controls in advance.
