AEHR - historical reconstruction, 30 Mar to 4 Jun 2026

AEHR rose 287.2 per cent between 30 March and 4 June 2026, from an adjusted close of 30.11 to 116.58. This note reconstructs what was datable before the close of 6 April, and how one defined-risk option structure was quoted through the move.

Every claim below is tied to a dated session and taken from exchange records - adjusted exchange prices and end-of-day option quotations as recorded - and the calculations were repeated from the dated records. The episode appears here because it instructs, not because it represents.

Observed before

What was knowable beforehand

The reconstruction dates its entry to the close of 6 April 2026 - one day before the report. The report's content was not knowable. Its approximate date was.

The company kept a rigid reporting cadence: the third-quarter reports of the two prior fiscal years had landed on 8 April 2025 and 9 April 2024. So the first week of April was a datable window for the Q3 FY26 report before any announcement appeared. Separately, the preceding quarter, reported on 8 January 2026, had carried an EPS beat of +50 per cent - a dated fact about the direction the business was already surprising in.

Against that calendar stood the tape: the 30 March undercut at 29.13, the 31 March reversal of 23.1 per cent on 2.2 times median volume, the 2 April close near the range highs, and then the 6 April close at 52.16 - a break of the entire March range on roughly five times median volume, the day before the datable report window.

A range break the day before a datable report does not make two signals. One: the market front-running an event whose timing was already public in outline.

Evidence gaps. The dated record used here identifies no news items for the ticker before 7 April, so the surrounding semiconductor-test narrative is treated as narrative and excluded. The evidence boundary below covers volatility.

Observed after

What happened

The window opens at a failure. On 30 March 2026 the shares undercut the low of their March range, printing a low of 29.13 and closing at 30.11. The next session, 31 March, reversed: a gain of 23.1 per cent to a close of 37.08 on 3.33 million shares, 2.2 times the median. By 2 April the close, at 44.32, sat near the March highs. On 6 April the stock closed at 52.16 on 7.93 million shares - roughly five times median volume - above the entire March range, whose top had been 46.95 on 4 March.

The following day, 7 April, the company reported quarterly results, with an EPS surprise of +37.5 per cent. The advance that followed ran for two months. On 2 June the shares gained 22 per cent; on 3 June they printed an all-time high of 121.80. The study window closes on 4 June at 116.58.

How a defined-risk expression behaved

The study takes a single long call in a back-month expiry placed through the dated report: the 18 June 2026 monthly 60 call, a listed strike whose spread, at 4.5 per cent of mid, was the cleanest in the chain. All fills cross the end-of-day quoted spread - buying at the ask, selling at the bid.

Interpretation

The discipline

The composite signal deserves a precise name. A five-times-volume break of an entire monthly range, one day before a report whose window three years of cadence had dated, is the market pricing a known event early. The fitting expression is a back-month call carried through the event: it participates in the gap if the report confirms, and in the trend that follows, without depending on either arriving quickly. At 74 days to expiry and a delta of 0.513, the structure was not a lottery ticket on the report; it had materially lower odds of expiring worthless than a far out-of-the-money alternative, and no leverage beyond its premium.

The quieter lesson concerns dating. An earnings calendar entry is not the only legitimate way to date a catalyst. A rigid reporting cadence - here, the first week of April in three consecutive years - is itself dated information, available before any press release. It dates a window rather than a day - exactly what a back-month expiry exists to tolerate.

Evidence boundary

Evidence notes

The available record does not establish implied-volatility behaviour. Whether the options market showed a volatility spike or term-structure inversion before entry is unverified. The classification rests on price, volume and the calendar.

The cadence argument dates a window, not a date. No press release announcing 7 April appears in the dated record used here - it holds no news for the ticker before that session - so the claim of datability is probabilistic, and honest use of it required an expiry long enough to absorb a report arriving a week or two later than the cadence suggested.

The 4 June endpoint closes the candidate window; it carries no special standing as a signal. Holding to the 18 June expiry would have left roughly 52.6 of intrinsic value against a 17 June spot of 112.62, so the shape of the outcome is not an artifact of the endpoint chosen; but the exact figures quoted above are.

Two facts run against the episode's tidiness. Insider filings show the chief executive (Erickson), the chief financial officer (Siu) and directors selling or filing Form 4 transactions between 27 April and 15 May at prices of 88 to 108 - against the direction of the move through the second half of the window. And the report's revenue surprise ran negative, at -5 per cent: the beat rested on EPS only, and orders and the AI narrative, rather than clean reported numbers, carried the continuation. Finally, the move's occurrence selected the episode; selection of that kind flatters any method, and the note makes no claim of frequency or repeatability.
