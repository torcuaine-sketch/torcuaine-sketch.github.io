Source record

Each entry in the series asks one question of one episode: which parts of a large move carried a date before the move happened. The entries quote the dated prices and the quoted option markets; this index does neither. It describes classes of fact and patterns of recurrence, and it deliberately contains no return figures — the anatomy of knowability does not require them. It is a study of public market history. It is not a record of firm activity, and nothing below describes a trade the firm executed or a position the firm held.

What the series contains

Twenty reconstructions, each confined to one name and one window inside the April–July 2026 study period. Sixteen reconstruct advances; four — SOC, RDW, NVTS and CAR — reconstruct declines, because knowability is symmetric and a method that can only read one direction is half a method. The spectrum is wide on purpose: megacap turnarounds (INTC, AMD) sit beside small-capitalisation parabolas (RXT, MXL), a relisted post-reorganisation semiconductor (WOLF), a pre-revenue story stock in a distress chain (SOC), and a call-driven squeeze in a vehicle-rental company (CAR).

The evidentiary basis is uniform across all twenty. Prices are adjusted daily exchange records. Option quotations are end-of-day chains as recorded, and every fill is crossed in full — entry at the ask, exit at the bid — so that no entry benefits from a midpoint it could not have dealt at. Every sequence was adversarially recomputed after assembly. Where a record could not be verified — an implied-volatility field the endpoint did not serve, a news table empty before the event — the entry says so in its limitations rather than filling the gap with narrative.

What recurs

Four patterns of knowability repeat across the series, in both directions of the tape.

The first is the structural break out of the April panic with a dated report three to five weeks ahead. In INTC, ALAB, ARM, NBIS, HUT and STX the same configuration appears: a base or 52-week-high level that was public for weeks, broken on expanded volume in early or mid-April, with a quarterly report whose approximate date was inferable from reporting cadence alone. Nothing in any of these entries depends on catching the low of the window; each enters after the break and before the catalyst, and the break itself is the signal.

The second is implied volatility rising together with the breakout — the chase regime. AMD is the clean instance: the thirty-day measure climbed as the price cleared its range, with the front month over the back, which reads as call demand expanding into the move rather than being sold into it. At that confluence the expression is the uncapped back-month call; capping the wing with a spread is a hedge against the wrong risk.

The third is the volatility crush after the report, on a breakout that has already confirmed itself. WOLF is the reference anatomy: the event premium unloaded on the session the reversal printed a new high on record volume, leaving calls cheaper than the volatility that subsequently materialised. OSCR is the same pattern entered the morning after the print. The rule buys premium on the crush and leaves it on the spike.

The fourth runs the other way: the broken parabola with a dated event ahead. RDW, NVTS and CAR share a trigger discipline — not the first red day, but the failed retest, the reversal bar on record volume — and a common expression: back-month puts, never the front week, because a retrace of this kind needs runway more than it needs precision. SOC extends the pattern to an undated catalyst: a distress chain telegraphed the forced raise without dating it, and there the expiry is the thesis — three to four weeks of life, never the coming Friday.

A fifth constant is quieter but spans all twenty entries: the instrument is always a single listed option in a back month that outlives the catalyst with margin, struck near the money, with no spread overlay anywhere in the series. The maximum loss of every structure studied is the premium paid, and nothing more.

What the series is honest about

Three honesties are structural, and the reader should hold all three. First, every name was selected because the move occurred. No entry estimates how often comparable setups resolve badly, and no frequency claim is made anywhere in the series. Second, exit windows carry hindsight, and the entries label it: study-window boundaries that happened to be kind, blow-off sessions that happened to be near tops, timing luck admitted without qualification. Where an exit rule failed at the exact bar it was designed for, the entry corrects the rule in public rather than defending it.

Third, the series argues that the exit matters more than the entry. The ALAB anatomy is the clearest case: the chosen expiration ended the position mechanically and thereby prevented overstaying the slide that followed — the expiry, written before the position existed, did the work a discretionary exit would have been asked to do at the worst moment. The entries that entered far above their window lows make the same point from the other side: knowability, not bottom-fishing, is what the evidence dated.

How to read each entry

Each entry is the anatomy of one episode, built to a fixed grammar. It opens with what happened, in dated sessions. It then separates what was knowable beforehand — every element of the entry case dated on or before the entry session — from what only became knowable after. It prints the instrument's quotations at entry and at exit, crossed in full, from the chains as they stood. And it closes with its limitations: the fields that could not be verified, the records that were empty, the dates inferred from cadence rather than from a calendar row, the luck components named as luck.

Read the limitations as part of the evidence, not as boilerplate. And read every entry as what it declares itself to be: a study of public market history. None of the twenty is a record of activity of the firm, none describes a position held by the firm, and none implies a return. The series exists to show what the dated record contained before the fact — not to claim what anyone did with it.
