Company financing and security behaviour

An industry label tells you what a company sells. It does not tell you what its security does when funding tightens, and that second question is the only one a portfolio has to answer.

Type
Essay
Published
May 2026

A sector classification is a statement about revenue. A portfolio needs a statement about behaviour: what this instrument does when real rates rise, when credit spreads gap, when correlation collapses toward one. Those two statements agree far less often than the convenience of sector labels suggests, and the disagreement is where concentration risk hides.

The failure of the label

Two companies in the same sector can sit on opposite sides of every regime that matters. A capital-light software business funded from cash flow and a leveraged roll-up in the same classification will diverge sharply the moment refinancing becomes the binding constraint. A portfolio that treats them as one exposure is not diversified between them; it simply cannot see the difference.

The inverse is more dangerous. Instruments in unrelated sectors frequently share a single dominant driver - long-duration cash flows, dollar funding, a commodity input - and a sector-balanced portfolio can therefore be a concentrated bet on that driver while appearing spread across the economy. This is the error that shows up only in the event that reveals it.

A sector-balanced portfolio can be a concentrated bet, while appearing to be spread across the economy.

What an archetype is

An archetype is a behavioural class: a grouping of instruments that respond the same way to the same regime shift, inferred from conditional behaviour rather than assigned from a taxonomy. The classification is causal in intent - we are asking what drives this instrument, not what it correlates with - and correlation alone is explicitly insufficient, because two instruments can correlate for a season without sharing any driver at all.

InputQuestion it answersWhy it is not sufficient alone
Regime responseHow does this behave when the regime changes stateRegimes are inferred, so the label inherits that uncertainty
Funding sensitivityWhat happens when the cost or availability of funding movesQuiet in periods when funding is abundant
Duration of cash flowsWhere in time the value sitsSays nothing about credit or convexity
Conditional correlationWhat it moves with, and only under which stateUnstable, and easily mistaken for causation

Where the classification is allowed to act

An archetype does not size a position by itself. It enters the process at two points and is constrained at both.

  1. ViewsRegime-conditional views are expressed at the archetype level rather than the instrument level, so a view is a statement about a behaviour class, not a forecast about one security.
  2. Risk clusteringHierarchical risk parity clusters on archetype behaviour, so diversification is measured across drivers instead of across labels.
  3. ConstraintExposure limits apply per archetype. An archetype that has drifted or is thinly populated is capped, because a class inferred from few members is a guess wearing a category name.

How the classification is falsified

A classification that cannot be wrong is not a classification, it is a narrative. Each assignment is re-derived nightly on the current regime estimate and re-tested through the rolling walk-forward window, so a label that only held in the period that produced it is removed rather than defended. Instruments whose archetype is unstable across the window are treated as unclassified, and unclassified is a size cap, not a shrug.

Three separate models test the current assignment before any allocation based on it is committed. The review is designed to expose classifications fitted to the sample rather than inferred from it.

Scope

Behavioural classification does not predict returns, and a correctly identified archetype can still be a poor investment. The claim is narrower and, we think, more defensible: that measuring diversification across drivers rather than across labels gives a more honest picture of what a portfolio is actually exposed to. Whether that picture leads to a better outcome in any given period is not something this note asserts.
